The Science of the human mind and behavior

Most people see what is in front of them. I want to know what it means.

I am Shrutartha Rakshit. I write about patterns, contradictions, human behaviour, and the small details that do not fit the first version of a story. Some cases are personal; others reach me through a friend or a peer who asks me to take a second look.

I describe myself as altruistic, a high-functioning sociopath, and a pseudo polymath.The more useful question is what can be observed: how I reason, what I notice, where my theories fail, and whether I am willing to revise them.

Working rule: an elegant theory is still only a theory until the evidence supports it.

Case 001 · Personal record · Aug–Sep 2026

The conversation that was not supposed to exist

The relationship appeared intact until inconsistencies around contact with a former boyfriend became impossible to ignore. I learned that my ex girlfriend had been communicating with her ex around August or September 2026. The contact had not been disclosed to me, and the conversations were hidden or deleted. Later, she acknowledged that she had concealed the chats because she knew she was hiding them.

The important detail was not simply that two people had spoken. It was the gap between what I believed was happening, what was being kept from me, and what the available records showed. I had shared a conversation with a classmate openly; the contact with her ex, as I understood it, had been concealed. That asymmetry changed how I interpreted the situation.

I kept the distinction between evidence and conclusion in view: the concealed messages and later admissions were evidence of secrecy; what every exchange meant, or what either person privately intended, could not be established from that fact alone. After the breakup, I also saw that she had unblocked and followed him. That was a later event, not proof by itself of every suspicion I had during the relationship.

File 002 · Human behaviour

People are not puzzles with one correct answer

Behaviour is shaped by motive, context, habit, fear, loyalty, and chance. I am interested in what people do and the gap between their stated reasons and their observable choices. But a convincing explanation is not proof, and no one can reliably read another mind from a single gesture.

File 003 · The discipline

Intelligence without correction becomes theatre

The temptation is to build a theory that makes the observer look brilliant. The discipline is to record the detail that disproves it. A good investigator does not merely collect confirmations; he actively searches for the fact that could make him wrong.

Case 004 · Brought to me by a friend

The transfer that never arrived

A friend came to me after a small group dinner turned awkward. One person insisted he had already sent his share and even showed a payment screen. The others were about to call him a liar, but I wanted to know exactly what the screen showed before deciding what it meant.

The status said “processing”, not “successful”. The next morning, the transaction history showed no completed payment. When my friend asked neutrally whether the transfer had gone through, the story changed: first the app was blamed, then the person admitted he had not actually confirmed it. It was a modest lie about a modest sum, but the sequence mattered. I could establish that the payment had not arrived; I could not establish whether the original plan had been deliberate deception or an attempt to save face until the explanation changed.

Case 005 · Brought to me by a peer

The account with somebody else’s face

A friend asked me to look at an Instagram account belonging to someone he had been messaging for a few weeks. The account had a believable mix of selfies, café photos, and stories about college, but small details kept shifting: the person described two different years of study and gave inconsistent answers about a campus event they claimed to have attended together.

I did not try to trace a private person or dig for personal information. I checked only the public photographs. One portrait appeared on an older, public creator account with a different name and years of posts predating the profile my friend had found. That did not tell me who was operating the new account, but it made the claimed identity unreliable. I told my friend not to send money, intimate images, documents, or location details, and to ask for a brief live call if he wanted one final, ordinary check. The account avoided it. My conclusion was narrow: the photos had been reused and the identity had not been verified. I could not responsibly claim to know who was behind it.

Case 006 · Brought to me by a classmate

The résumé that grew in every retelling

A friend was helping organise a college event and asked me about a volunteer who introduced himself as the founder of a student initiative and the lead organiser of a previous inter-college programme. His résumé sounded impressive, but the titles changed depending on the audience. In one conversation he had founded the initiative; in another, he had joined it after it was already running.

I compared the claims with the event poster and the initiative’s public page, then suggested my friend ask the previous organiser what each person had actually done. The records showed that the volunteer had contributed to one event, but had not led it, and the initiative had been started by someone else. That was enough to correct the role description. I did not assume every part of his personality was fake or announce a grand theory about his motives. I had found an inflated claim, not a complete map of a person.

Field notes

Margin

Current working hypothesis

The smallest detail is useful only when it changes the larger explanation.

Rules of deduction

  • Separate observation from interpretation.
  • Keep at least two competing explanations.
  • Look for disconfirming evidence.
  • Say “unknown” when it is unknown.
  • Never mistake confidence for accuracy.

Areas of interest

Human psychology · Logic · Literature · Science · Systems · Narrative structure · The art of asking better questions.

My method

Observation → inference → test

01 / Observe

Record what is actually present before deciding what it means. Details, timing, inconsistencies, and context go into the notebook. Assumptions stay outside the evidence column.

02 / Infer

Build a small set of explanations that could account for the facts. Prefer the explanation that fits the evidence with the fewest unsupported assumptions, but do not confuse simplicity with truth.

03 / Test

Ask what else should be true if a theory is correct. Search for counterexamples. Update the theory when new information arrives, even when the old version felt satisfying.

04 / Conclude

Label conclusions by confidence: established, plausible, or speculative. A detective's job is not to sound certain. It is to get closer to what happened.

The mind behind the notes

Personal profile

Altruism / The human variable

I value helping people, but good intentions are not a substitute for understanding what someone actually needs. Compassion can coexist with analysis. The point is to understand without reducing people to case studies.

Emotional distance / A hypothesis, not a diagnosis

I sometimes describe myself as a “high-functioning sociopath,” borrowing the language of fiction to describe a detached, analytical self-image. It is not a medical diagnosis. A more precise account should focus on specific behaviours, emotions, choices, and their effects.

Pseudo-polymathy / Many doors, one notebook

I am curious across several fields and enjoy connecting ideas. Curiosity is the beginning, not proof of mastery. Each field demands its own study, practice, and humility before I can claim expertise.

The detective's flaw

Pattern-seeking can reveal connections, but it can also invent them. I have to watch for confirmation bias, overconfidence, and the urge to turn people into predictable characters. The strongest mind is willing to correct itself.

Notebook / Unfinished thoughts

Open entries
Entry 01 · Logic

What would change my mind?

Before defending a conclusion, identify the evidence that would force a revision. If no possible evidence could change the theory, it is no longer an investigation.

Entry 02 · Psychology

Explanation is not justification

Understanding why someone acted a certain way does not automatically excuse the action. Motive, impact, and responsibility are separate questions.

Entry 03 · Self-study

The observer is part of the experiment

My mood, expectations, memory, and preferences affect what I notice. Self-awareness is not a dramatic monologue; it is another layer of quality control.

Entry 04 · The unknown

Leave a question open

Not every mystery has enough information for a responsible answer. Sometimes the most accurate entry in the notebook is simply: insufficient evidence.

Open sealed file / Case 001 chronology and evidentiary limits

Known from my account and the records I described: I discovered that ex girlfriend had been in contact with her former boyfriend around August or September 2026; the contact was not disclosed to me, and chats were hidden or deleted. She later acknowledged hiding those chats. After the breakup, she unblocked and followed him.

Why it mattered: the issue was not that any conversation with an ex automatically proves betrayal. It was the concealment, the deleted record, and the mismatch between the transparency I offered and the transparency I received. Those facts damaged trust.

What this file does not claim: it does not assign an exact duration beyond what I can verify, claim to know private motives, or treat a later follow as proof of every possible theory. The record supports a conclusion about secrecy and broken trust; anything beyond the evidence remains an inference.

Method note: preserve the timeline, distinguish direct evidence from interpretation, and do not let anger write the conclusion before the facts do.

Case 004 · The transfer that never arrived

What my friend told me: after a group dinner, one person said he had paid his share and showed a payment screen as proof.

What I checked: the screen showed a pending transaction, not a successful one. The next morning, the payment history still showed no completed transfer. I asked my friend to check the status rather than accuse anyone based on a screenshot.

What changed: the person first blamed the app, then admitted he had not completed the payment. I could establish that the money had not arrived. I could not know his original intention until his own explanation changed.

My finding: the claim that payment was complete was false. The amount was small; the useful lesson was to verify the status before turning suspicion into certainty.

Case 005 · The account with somebody else’s face

What my friend told me: he had been messaging an Instagram account whose biography and stories seemed plausible, but details about college and shared events kept changing.

What I checked: I looked only at publicly available photographs. One image appeared on an older public account belonging to a creator using a different name. That supported the possibility that the image had been reused, not the identity of whoever controlled the newer account.

What happened next: my friend stopped sharing personal details and suggested a brief live call. The account avoided verification. He ended contact and reported it through the platform.

My finding: the claimed identity was unverified and at least one photo appeared to have been taken from elsewhere,probably printerest. I did not try to identify, locate, or expose the person behind the account. The safest conclusion was also the most defensible one: do not trust a profile whose identity cannot be verified.

Case 006 · The résumé that grew in every retelling

What my friend told me: a volunteer claimed to have founded a student initiative and led a previous inter-college event, but the story shifted depending on who was listening.

What I checked: the public event poster and initiative page credited different people. My friend then asked the previous organiser to clarify the volunteer’s actual role, without demanding private messages or confidential information.

What the records supported: the volunteer had contributed, but had not led the event and had not founded the initiative.

My finding: two credentials had been exaggerated. That did not prove that every part of his character was false, nor did it tell me why he had done it. I corrected the specific claims and left the rest alone.
